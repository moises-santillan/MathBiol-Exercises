\section{General Model Development}

Gene expression is a complex process involving numerous chemical reactions at each step of transcription and translation. However, we can build a simple model based on the central dogma of molecular biology.

Consider a basic operon whose genes code for an enzyme $E$ that catalyzes the synthesis of a metabolite $P$. The synthesis of the operon's mRNA, $M$, via transcription is feedback-regulated by $P$. Additionally, the activity of the $E$ enzymes may also be feedback-regulated by $P$. With these considerations, the dynamics of $M$, $E$, and $P$ are governed by the following ordinary differential equations (ODEs):
\begin{eqnarray}
\dot{M} &=& k_M f(P) - \gamma_M M, \label{eq:27.01}\\
\dot{E} &=& k_E M - \gamma_E E,\label{eq:27.02}\\
\dot{P} &=& k_P g(P) E - \gamma_P P, \label{eq:27.03}
\end{eqnarray}
Here, $k_M$ represents the maximum transcription initiation rate, $\gamma_M$ is the rate constant for mRNA degradation, $k_E$ denotes the translation initiation rate, $\gamma_E$ is the rate constant for enzyme degradation, $k_P$ is the maximum metabolite synthesis rate per enzyme molecule, and $\gamma_P$ is the rate constant for metabolite degradation. The functions $f(P)$ and $g(P)$ denote feedback regulation in terms of metabolite concentration. $f(P)$ can be interpreted as the probability that the promoter is not repressed, while $g(P)$ represents the fraction of un-inhibited enzyme molecules. These functions are decreasing (increasing) functions of $P$ in case of negative (positive) feedback regulation.

Typically, the half-life of mRNA molecules is on the order of a few minutes, while that of enzymes can range from a few hours to days, and that of metabolites is generally on the order of a few hours. Considering these timescales and the fact that the half-life times ($\tau_x$) of the model variables are determined by their degradation rate constants ($\tau_x = \ln(2)/\gamma_x$), we can make a quasi-steady-state approximation for the variable $M$ to simplify the model. Assuming $dM/dt \approx 0$, we get:
\begin{equation*}
M \approx \frac{k_M}{\gamma_M} f(P).
\end{equation*}
With this approximation, the reduced model becomes:
\begin{eqnarray}
\dot{E} &=& \kappa_E f(P) - \gamma_E E,\label{eq:27.04}\\
\dot{P} &=& k_P g(P) E - \gamma_P P, \label{eq:27.05}
\end{eqnarray}
where $\kappa_E=k_M k_E / \gamma_M$ is the maximum rate of enzyme synthesis.

The functions $f(P)$ and $g(P)$ take values in the interval $[0, 1]$ as they can be understood as probabilities. $f(P)$ is the probability that the mRNA polymerase binding site is not blocked by a repressor molecule, which depends on the amount of active repressor molecules. Depending on the system, repressor molecules are active when they are free from or bound by specific metabolites. In summary, transcriptional repression can be reduced to a, more or less intricate, ligand-receptor interaction. Thus, based on the results in Chapter \ref{ch24}, the function $f(P)$ can be approximated by a growing or decreasing Hill function, depending on whether it accounts for positive or negative feedback regulation:
\begin{equation*}
f(P)=\frac{1}{2} \frac{(K_f^{n_f}+P^{n_f})\pm(K_f^{n_f}-P^{n_f})}{K_f^{n_f}+P^{n_f}}.
\end{equation*}
In the equation above, $K_f$ is the half-saturation constant ($f(K_f)=1/2$), and $n_f \geq 1$ is the so-called Hill exponent. It measures the sigmoidicity of the Hill function. When $n_f=1$, the function $f(P)$ becomes a Michaelis-Menten equation, whereas it approaches a step function when $n_f \to \infty$. The sign $+$ ($-$) is chosen to account for negative (positive) regulation.

Enzymes are often inhibited or activated when they are bound by specific metabolites. Thus, enzyme inhibition can also be reduced to a ligand-receptor problem, and the function $g(P)$ can be approximated by a Hill function:
\begin{equation*}
g(P)=\frac{1}{2} \frac{(K_g^{n_g}+P^{n_g})\pm(K_g^{n_g}-P^{n_g})}{K_g^{n_g}+P^{n_g}}.
\end{equation*}

\section{Redundant Negative Feedback regulation}

A specific model for the tryptophan operon, discussed in the previous chapter, can be derived from our general model as follows:
\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{dE}{dt}&=&\kappa_E f(T) - \gamma_E E, \label{eq:27.06}\\
\frac{dT}{dt}&=&k_T g(T) E - \gamma_T T, \label{eq:27.07}
\end{eqnarray}
with
\begin{eqnarray*}
f(T)&=&\frac{K_E^{n_E}}{K_E^{n_E}+T^{n_E}},\\
g(T)&=&\frac{K_T^{n_T}}{K_T^{n_T}+T^{n_T}}.
\end{eqnarray*}
In these equations, $T$ represents the intracellular concentration of tryptophan, the amino acid produced by the tryptophan operon. $E$ denotes the concentration of the enzyme anthranilate synthase, a tetrameric protein composed of two peptides resulting from the expression of the first gene (trpE) and two peptides from the second gene (trpD) of the tryptophan operon. Anthranilate synthase catalyzes the rate-limiting step in tryptophan biosynthesis, and its activity is feedback-regulated by the end product, tryptophan.

The first equation describes the dynamics of anthranilate synthase concentration, where $\kappa_E$ is the maximum rate of enzyme synthesis, $K_E$ is the half-saturation constant for tryptophan's negative feedback on enzyme expression, and $n_E$ is the Hill coefficient governing the cooperativity of this feedback regulation.

The second equation models the dynamics of tryptophan concentration, where $k_T$ is the maximum rate of tryptophan synthesis, $K_T$ is the half-saturation constant for tryptophan's negative feedback on its own synthesis, $n_T$ is the corresponding Hill coefficient, and $\gamma_T$ is the rate constant for tryptophan degradation or consumption.

This model captures the essential features of the tryptophan operon, including the multi-layered negative feedback regulation mechanisms employed to control tryptophan biosynthesis. Firstly, the transcription of the operon is negatively regulated by tryptophan through two distinct mechanisms: repression and transcriptional attenuation, both of which are accounted for by a single regulatory function. Secondly, the activity of the key enzyme anthranilate synthase is directly inhibited by tryptophan through allosteric regulation. 

One of the first questions that arises is the reason for redundant negative feedback mechanisms in the tryptophan operon system. We address this question by analyzing the stability of the system's steady states.

To find the steady states, we impose the conditions $dE/dt=0$ and $dT/dt=0$. From the first condition, we get:
\begin{equation}
E = \frac{\kappa_E}{\gamma_E} \frac{K_E^{n_E}}{K_E^{n_E}+T^{n_E}} . \label{eq:27.08}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, the condition $dT/dt=0$ implies:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{k_T}{\gamma_T} \frac{K_T^{n_T}}{K_T^{n_T}+T^{n_T}} E . \label{eq:27.09}
\end{equation}
By substituting Eq. \refeq{eq:27.09} into Eq. \refeq{eq:27.08}, we obtain an implicit equation for the steady-state tryptophan concentration:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\kappa_E k_T}{\gamma_E \gamma_T} \frac{K_E^{n_E}}{K_E^{n_E}+T^{n_E}} \frac{K_T^{n_T}}{K_T^{n_T}+T^{n_T}}. \label{eq:27.10}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side of Eq. \refeq{eq:27.10} is a monotonically increasing linear function of $T$ (the 45-degree diagonal line). On the other hand, the right-hand side is the product of two decreasing Hill functions, making it a decreasing function of $T$ that takes values in the interval $[0, \kappa_E k_T/\gamma_E \gamma_T]$.

It can be proven that the two curves intersect at a single point, implying that the ODE system has a unique steady state. The stationary value of $T$ ($T^*$) is given by the solution of Eq. \refeq{eq:27.10}. Furthermore, the steady-state value of $E$ ($E^*$) can be computed by substituting $T^*$ into Eq. \refeq{eq:27.08}.

To analyze the stability of the steady state, we linearize the system around the steady state $(E^*, T^*)$ by defining the perturbations $\delta E = E - E^*$ and $\delta T = T - T^*$. The linearized system is given by:
\begin{eqnarray}
\frac{d(\delta E)}{dt} &=& - \gamma_E \delta E + \kappa_E f'(T^*) \delta T, \label{eq:27.11}\\
\frac{d(\delta T)}{dt} &=& k_T g(T^*) \delta E + \left( k_T g'(T^*) E^* - \gamma_T \right) \delta T. \label{eq:27.12}
\end{eqnarray}
